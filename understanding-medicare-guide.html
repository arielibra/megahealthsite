Understanding Medicare: A Comprehensive Guide

Reviewed by Dr. Sam Borukhov, PharmD
Owner & Supervising Pharmacist · Reviewed October 21, 2026

Medicare is federal health insurance for people 65 and older, certain younger people with disabilities, and people with End-Stage Renal Disease. In practical terms, Medicare helps pay for hospital care, doctor visits, outpatient services, preventive care, and, if you choose drug coverage, prescription medications. The most important decision is not simply whether to enroll, but how you want to receive your coverage: through Original Medicare or through a private Medicare Advantage plan approved by Medicare. (medicare.gov)

This guide explains what Medicare is, how the main parts work, when Medicare eligibility begins, and how to compare Medicare plans without getting lost in jargon. It also covers current enrollment timing, including the October 15–December 7 Open Enrollment window that is underway in 2026 for 2027 coverage. (medicare.gov)

What is Medicare?

Medicare is a federal health insurance program that helps eligible people pay for covered health care services. It is not one single plan; it is a system with parts, choices, enrollment periods, premiums, deductibles, copayments, networks, and private-plan options that can change from year to year. (medicare.gov)

At the center of Medicare are Part A and Part B, often called Original Medicare. Part A generally helps cover inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility care, hospice care, and some home health care. Part B generally helps cover doctor services, outpatient care, durable medical equipment, home health care, and many preventive services. (medicare.gov)

From there, beneficiaries usually make additional choices. Some people keep Original Medicare and add a stand-alone Part D prescription drug plan, and some also buy Medicare Supplement Insurance, known as Medigap, to help with out-of-pocket costs. Others choose Medicare Advantage, also called Part C, which is offered by private companies approved by Medicare and usually bundles Part A, Part B, and often Part D into one plan. (medicare.gov)

Defining characteristics of Medicare include:

  • Federal foundation: Medicare is a federal program, not a family or employer group plan.

  • Individual coverage: Medicare does not offer family plans; each eligible person makes individual coverage choices.

  • Multiple parts: Part A, Part B, Part C, and Part D each serve different roles.

  • Annual review: Medicare health and drug plans can change costs, benefits, networks, and formularies each year.

  • Private-plan options: Medicare Advantage, Part D, and Medigap policies are offered by private insurers under Medicare rules.

  • Enrollment rules: You can enroll, switch, or drop certain coverage only during specific enrollment periods unless you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period. (medicare.gov)

Medicare’s main parts and what they cover

Understanding Medicare starts with understanding its parts. The letters are easy to mix up, but each one answers a different coverage question: hospital care, medical care, prescription drugs, or an all-in-one alternative to Original Medicare.

Part A covers hospital-related care

Medicare Part A is hospital insurance. It helps pay for inpatient hospital care, skilled nursing facility care after a qualifying hospital stay, hospice care, and some home health services. It does not mean every long-term care need is covered, and it does not replace custodial nursing home care. (medicare.gov)

Many people do not pay a monthly premium for Part A because they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes long enough while working. Even when the monthly premium is $0, Part A still has cost-sharing. For example, the 2026 Part A inpatient hospital deductible is $1,736 for each benefit period, not simply once per calendar year. (medicare.gov)

Part B covers medical and outpatient care

Medicare Part B is medical insurance. It helps cover services from doctors and other health care providers, outpatient care, durable medical equipment such as walkers or wheelchairs, many preventive services, and certain home health services. (medicare.gov)

Part B usually has a monthly premium, an annual deductible, and coinsurance after the deductible. If you delay Part B when you are first eligible and do not have qualifying coverage through current employment, you may face a late enrollment penalty. That penalty can last as long as you have Part B, so timing matters. (medicare.gov)

Part C is Medicare Advantage

Medicare Advantage, or Part C, is an alternative way to receive Medicare benefits. Instead of using Original Medicare directly, you enroll in a Medicare-approved private plan. Medicare Advantage plans must cover all emergency and urgent care and almost all medically necessary services that Original Medicare covers, and many plans include prescription drug coverage. (medicare.gov)

The tradeoff is that Medicare Advantage plans have plan-specific rules. You may need to use a provider network, get referrals, request prior authorization for certain services, or pay different amounts depending on whether a provider is in network. These details vary by plan type, such as HMO, PPO, PFFS, SNP, or MSA. (medicare.gov)

Part D covers prescription drugs

Medicare Part D helps cover prescription drugs, including many recommended vaccines. You can get Part D through a stand-alone drug plan if you use Original Medicare, or through a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage. Private insurance companies run Part D plans, but they must follow Medicare rules. (medicare.gov)

Part D costs depend on the plan, the medications you take, the pharmacy you use, and whether your drugs are on the plan’s formulary. In 2026, no Medicare drug plan may have a deductible higher than $615, and out-of-pocket spending for covered Part D drugs is capped at $2,100 for the year. For 2027, Medicare.gov shows those figures increasing to a $700 maximum deductible and a $2,400 out-of-pocket threshold for covered Part D drugs. (medicare.gov)

Medigap helps with Original Medicare cost-sharing

Medigap is not a Medicare “part,” but it is an important coverage option. Medicare Supplement Insurance, or Medigap, is extra insurance you buy from a private company to help pay your share of out-of-pocket costs under Original Medicare, such as copayments, coinsurance, and deductibles. You generally must have both Part A and Part B to buy Medigap. (medicare.gov)

Medigap works only with Original Medicare, not Medicare Advantage. If you have a Medigap policy and receive covered care, Medicare pays its share of the approved amount first, and then the Medigap policy pays according to its benefits. Medigap policies are standardized in most states, which means the benefits for the same lettered plan are the same regardless of the company selling it, though premiums can differ. (medicare.gov)

Who qualifies for Medicare?

Medicare eligibility generally begins at age 65, but younger people may qualify if they have certain disabilities or End-Stage Renal Disease. Eligibility can also depend on work history, Social Security or Railroad Retirement Board benefits, disability status, and specific medical conditions. (medicare.gov)

For many people, the first Medicare decision arrives around their 65th birthday. If you are already receiving Social Security benefits, you may be enrolled in Part A and Part B automatically. If not, you may need to sign up yourself during your Initial Enrollment Period. (medicare.gov)

People under 65 may qualify after receiving disability benefits for a required period, or because they have ALS or ESRD. ESRD means permanent kidney failure requiring dialysis or a kidney transplant. Because these situations involve specific rules, it is especially important to use Medicare.gov, Social Security, or a State Health Insurance Assistance Program for personalized guidance. (medicare.gov)

A simple way to think about Medicare eligibility is this: eligibility opens the door, but enrollment choices determine the coverage path. You may be eligible for Medicare but still need to decide whether to take Part B now, whether you need Part D, whether employer coverage changes your timing, and whether Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage better fits your care needs.

How does Medicare work once you enroll?

Medicare works by combining federal coverage rules with your chosen coverage path. First, you sign up for Part A and Part B if you need them. Then you choose how to receive your coverage: Original Medicare, usually with optional Part D and possibly Medigap, or Medicare Advantage through a private plan approved by Medicare. (medicare.gov)

With Original Medicare, you can generally use any doctor or hospital that accepts Medicare anywhere in the United States and U.S. territories listed by Medicare. You usually pay a share of costs as you receive services, and Original Medicare does not have a yearly out-of-pocket maximum unless you have other coverage, such as Medigap, Medicaid, employer, retiree, or union coverage. (medicare.gov)

With Medicare Advantage, you still have Medicare, but a private plan manages your benefits. These plans often include extra benefits Original Medicare does not cover, such as some dental, vision, or hearing benefits, but they can also limit care to a network or require prior authorization. Costs and benefits can vary widely by county, plan, and year. (medicare.gov)

A practical Medicare setup usually involves four questions:

  1. Do I need Part A and Part B now? Your age, work status, and other insurance affect timing.

  2. Do I want Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage? This choice affects provider access, plan rules, and out-of-pocket risk.

  3. How will I cover prescriptions? You may need Part D through a stand-alone plan or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage.

  4. How much cost-sharing can I handle? Original Medicare plus Medigap can reduce unpredictable cost-sharing, while Medicare Advantage uses plan-specific premiums, copays, networks, and an annual out-of-pocket limit for covered medical services.

Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage are different coverage paths

Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage both connect you to Medicare-covered care, but they work differently. The best choice depends on your doctors, prescriptions, travel patterns, budget, health conditions, and tolerance for network rules.

Original Medicare may appeal to people who want broad provider access, travel frequently within the U.S., or want the option to buy Medigap. Medicare Advantage may appeal to people who prefer a bundled plan, are comfortable using a network, and value extra benefits included by a specific local plan. Neither path is automatically best for everyone.

The key is to compare based on real usage, not advertisements. A plan with a low premium may cost more if your specialists are out of network, your drugs are placed on higher tiers, or important services require prior authorization. A Medigap policy may have a higher monthly premium, but it may reduce unpredictable cost-sharing under Original Medicare.

Medicare plans can change, so annual review matters

Medicare plans are not “set it and forget it.” Medicare Advantage and Part D plans can change their premiums, copays, provider networks, pharmacy networks, covered drugs, drug tiers, prior authorization rules, and extra benefits each year. Medicare encourages people to review current coverage before Open Enrollment and compare plan options for the upcoming year. (medicare.gov)

This annual review has become even more important because some insurers have reduced or exited Medicare Advantage offerings in certain markets. For example, the current news phrase “humana will exit plans affecting 600000 medicare advantage members” refers to reports that Humana planned to exit selected Medicare Advantage markets for 2027, affecting roughly 600,000 members. That does not mean every Humana member loses coverage, but it does show why members should read their Annual Notice of Change and any non-renewal notice carefully. (fiercehealthcare.com)

If your plan is ending, changing significantly, or leaving your county, do not assume the replacement option is the best fit. Check whether your doctors, hospitals, pharmacies, and medications are covered for the new year. If you receive a non-renewal notice, review the Special Enrollment Period information in the notice and compare options through Medicare.gov or 1-800-MEDICARE. Medicare.gov and 1-800-MEDICARE are official sources of unbiased Medicare information. (medicare.gov)

Review these items every fall:

  • Monthly premium for the coming year

  • Medical deductible, drug deductible, copays, and coinsurance

  • Maximum out-of-pocket limit for Medicare Advantage medical services

  • Whether your doctors and hospitals remain in network

  • Whether your prescriptions are still covered and on which tier

  • Whether your preferred pharmacy is still preferred or standard

  • Prior authorization, referral, and step therapy rules

  • Dental, vision, hearing, transportation, fitness, or over-the-counter benefits

  • Any Annual Notice of Change or plan termination letter

Medicare enrollment periods determine when changes can happen

Medicare enrollment periods are the windows when you can sign up for, switch, or drop certain coverage. Missing a window can lead to delayed coverage, late penalties, or fewer options, so dates matter.

The Initial Enrollment Period is your first chance to sign up for Medicare. For many people turning 65, it begins three months before the month they turn 65 and ends three months after that month. If you are still working and have qualifying employer coverage, different rules may apply, so you should confirm whether delaying Part B is safe before you do it. (medicare.gov)

Medicare Open Enrollment runs every year from October 15 through December 7. During this period, people with Medicare can join, drop, or switch Medicare Advantage plans, switch between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage, or join, drop, or switch Medicare drug plans. Changes made during Open Enrollment generally take effect January 1 of the next year. (medicare.gov)

Because today is October 21, 2026, Open Enrollment for 2027 coverage is currently underway. Medicare’s Plan Compare tool specifically prompts users to pick a 2027 plan from October 15 through December 7, 2026. (medicare.gov)

There is also a Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period from January 1 through March 31 for people already enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan. During that window, you can switch to another Medicare Advantage plan or return to Original Medicare and join a separate drug plan. You cannot use that period to switch from Original Medicare into Medicare Advantage or to change stand-alone Part D plans if you are in Original Medicare. (medicare.gov)

Special Enrollment Periods may be available after certain life events, such as moving, losing other coverage, gaining or losing Medicaid or Extra Help, or having your plan end. The exact rules vary by situation, so read official notices and confirm deadlines before assuming you can change plans later. (medicare.gov)

Costs depend on the coverage path you choose

Medicare costs include more than one monthly premium. A realistic budget should consider premiums, deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, prescription costs, out-of-network exposure, and services Medicare does not cover.

With Original Medicare, most people start with Part A and Part B. Part A may be premium-free for many beneficiaries, but it still has deductibles and coinsurance. Part B usually has a monthly premium and cost-sharing, and higher-income beneficiaries may pay more. (cms.gov)

If you add Part D, you pay the Part D plan’s premium, deductible if it has one, and prescription cost-sharing. If you add Medigap, you pay a separate Medigap premium in addition to the Part B premium and any Part D premium. Medigap can help pay Original Medicare cost-sharing, but it generally does not cover long-term care, routine dental or vision care, hearing aids, eyeglasses, or private-duty nursing. (medicare.gov)

With Medicare Advantage, costs depend on the specific plan. Some plans have low or $0 premiums, but you still must pay the Part B premium unless another program helps pay it. You may also pay copays or coinsurance as you use care, and costs can differ between in-network and out-of-network providers. (medicare.gov)

A useful way to compare costs is to estimate a normal year and a bad year. In a normal year, look at your expected office visits, prescriptions, labs, and routine care. In a bad year, consider hospitalization, specialist care, imaging, surgery, or expensive drugs. The plan that looks cheapest in a healthy year may not be the most comfortable if your care needs increase.

Prescription drug coverage deserves its own comparison

Drug coverage is one of the easiest places to make an expensive mistake. Two Medicare plans can look similar until you enter your specific medications, dosages, and pharmacies. The same drug may have a different tier, prior authorization rule, or pharmacy price from one plan to another.

Medicare drug plans use formularies, which are lists of covered drugs. They also use pharmacy networks, and Medicare.gov notes that preferred pharmacies usually offer the lowest drug costs. If you use an out-of-network pharmacy, you may have to pay the full cost. (medicare.gov)

Before choosing a Part D or Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage, check:

  • The exact drug name, dosage, and quantity

  • Whether the drug is covered by the plan

  • The drug tier and estimated monthly cost

  • Whether prior authorization, step therapy, or quantity limits apply

  • Whether your preferred pharmacy is preferred, standard, or out of network

  • Mail-order pricing if you use maintenance medications

  • The plan’s deductible and how it applies to your drugs

  • Whether your total drug costs could approach the annual Part D out-of-pocket cap

If you take no prescriptions today, drug coverage can still matter. Going without creditable prescription drug coverage when you are eligible may lead to a Part D late enrollment penalty later. Medicare calculates the Part D penalty using the national base beneficiary premium and the number of full uncovered months you were eligible but did not have Part D or other creditable drug coverage. (medicare.gov)

Online accounts help you manage Medicare plans

Many people use online portals to manage coverage after they enroll. Medicare.gov lets users log in to compare plans with saved drugs and pharmacies, review current coverage, and estimate plan costs. Private insurers also have member portals for claims, ID cards, plan documents, provider searches, and drug tools. (medicare.gov)

For example, someone searching “aetna medicare login” is usually looking for Aetna’s secure Medicare member website. Aetna says its Medicare member account can help members manage claims, check plan details, access policy documents, update contact or billing information, and use separate login paths for Medicare Advantage, Part D, and Medigap products. (aetna.com)

Use portals as management tools, not as the only source of truth. If something affects your care—such as whether a doctor is in network, whether a drug is covered, or whether prior authorization is required—confirm through the plan’s current-year documents or member services. Keep screenshots, confirmation numbers, and written notices when you make plan changes.

Common Medicare misconceptions

Medicare is familiar, but it is often misunderstood. Clearing up these misconceptions can prevent enrollment mistakes and surprise bills.

  • “Medicare is free.” Some people pay no Part A premium, but Part B, Part D, Medigap, Medicare Advantage cost-sharing, and uncovered services can still create costs.

  • “Medicare covers everything.” Medicare covers many medically necessary services, but it does not cover every service, item, or long-term care need.

  • “Medicare Advantage is the same as Medigap.” Medicare Advantage is an alternative way to receive Medicare benefits; Medigap supplements Original Medicare cost-sharing.

  • “My spouse’s Medicare choice applies to me.” Medicare is individual coverage, so spouses can choose different coverage paths.

  • “A $0 premium plan means $0 health care cost.” Premium is only one part of cost; copays, deductibles, networks, and drug costs still matter.

  • “If my plan was good last year, it will be good next year.” Plans can change annually, so review notices and compare options each fall.

  • “All doctors who take Medicare accept every Medicare Advantage plan.” Medicare Advantage plans have their own networks and rules.

  • “I can buy Medigap anytime with no questions.” Your one-time Medigap Open Enrollment Period begins when you are 65 or older and enrolled in Part B; after that, availability and pricing may be limited depending on state rules and circumstances. (medicare.gov)

Practical examples of how Medicare choices play out

Examples make Medicare easier to understand because the right setup depends on real circumstances.

Example 1: A frequent traveler

A person who spends several months a year in different states may value Original Medicare because it can be used with any provider that accepts Medicare across the U.S. They may add Part D for prescriptions and Medigap to help with out-of-pocket costs. This setup may cost more in monthly premiums but can offer broad provider access.

Example 2: A person with local doctors and predictable care

Someone who receives most care from local in-network doctors may find a Medicare Advantage plan appealing. The plan may include drug coverage and extra benefits, but the person should still verify that doctors, hospitals, prescriptions, and pharmacies are covered for the new plan year.

Example 3: A beneficiary whose plan is ending

If a Medicare Advantage plan will not renew, the member should compare new options during Open Enrollment or use any available Special Enrollment Period described in the plan notice. The replacement decision should not be based only on the plan name or premium; the member should confirm provider networks, drugs, costs, and prior authorization rules.

Example 4: A person taking expensive medications

A beneficiary with costly prescriptions should compare Part D or Medicare Advantage drug coverage with exact drug details. The 2026 Part D out-of-pocket cap for covered drugs offers important protection, but plan formularies, pharmacy networks, and utilization rules still affect what the person pays and how easily they can fill prescriptions. (medicare.gov)

Example 5: A new enrollee still working at 65

Someone covered by active employer insurance at 65 should ask whether that coverage allows them to delay Part B without penalty. Retiree coverage, COBRA, marketplace coverage, and active employer coverage can have different Medicare coordination rules, so this is not a place to guess.

A simple checklist for comparing Medicare plans

Comparing Medicare plans is easier when you use the same checklist for every option. Start with your personal care needs rather than the plan’s headline benefits.

  1. List your doctors and facilities. Include primary care, specialists, hospitals, labs, therapy providers, and preferred clinics.

  2. List your prescriptions. Add dosage, quantity, frequency, and preferred pharmacy.

  3. Choose your coverage path. Decide whether you are comparing Original Medicare plus Part D and possibly Medigap, Medicare Advantage plans, or both.

  4. Check total costs. Include premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, drug costs, and possible out-of-network costs.

  5. Confirm provider access. For Medicare Advantage, verify in-network status directly with the plan and provider.

  6. Review drug rules. Look for formulary status, tiers, prior authorization, step therapy, and pharmacy pricing.

  7. Read annual notices. Annual Notice of Change and Evidence of Coverage documents show what is changing.

  8. Consider health changes. New diagnoses, surgeries, medications, or specialists can change which plan is best.

  9. Check travel needs. If you spend time away from home, understand how routine, urgent, and emergency care works.

  10. Use official help. Medicare.gov, 1-800-MEDICARE, and local SHIP counselors can help you compare without sales pressure. (medicare.gov)

The bottom line on Medicare

Medicare is a federal health insurance program with several moving parts: Original Medicare, Medicare Advantage, Part D drug coverage, and Medigap. The core definition is simple, but the best choice depends on how you use care, which providers you need, which prescriptions you take, and how you prefer to balance monthly premiums against out-of-pocket risk.

For most people, the smartest Medicare habit is an annual review. In 2026, Open Enrollment runs from October 15 through December 7 for coverage beginning January 1, 2027, making this the right time to compare current benefits with next year’s options. Look past slogans, enter your real doctors and drugs, read plan notices carefully, and use official Medicare resources before you make a change. (medicare.gov)
