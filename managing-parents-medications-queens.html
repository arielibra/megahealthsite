Managing an Aging Parent’s Medications in Queens: A Practical Guide

Medically reviewed by Dr. Sam Borukhov, PharmD
Owner & Supervising Pharmacist · Reviewed September 29, 2026

If you help a parent manage their medications, four things make the biggest difference: one up-to-date medication list, one pharmacy that sees everything, refills synced to one day each month, and delivery so nobody has to make the trip.

1. Make one medication list

Include every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin and supplement, with the dose, time of day and prescribing doctor. Keep a copy on the fridge and one on your phone. Bring it to every appointment.

2. Use one pharmacy

When prescriptions are spread across several pharmacies and mail order, no one sees the full picture. One pharmacy can check for interactions and duplicates across everything. Transferring all of a parent’s prescriptions to us takes about a minute.

3. Get a medication review

Older adults are more sensitive to side effects like dizziness and confusion, which can lead to falls. A pharmacist medication review looks for medications that may no longer be needed, duplicates and interactions, and we share findings with the doctor.

4. Sync refills and set up delivery

We can line up refill dates so everything is ready on the same day each month, then deliver it free anywhere in NYC. You can live in Manhattan while your parent lives in Rego Park — we coordinate with you and deliver to them.

5. Organize the doses

  • A weekly pill organizer with morning and evening compartments works for many people.
  • Ask us about packaging options that make it easier to see whether a dose was taken.
  • Set phone alarms or use a simple reminder device.

6. Get permission to talk with the pharmacy

Ask your parent to tell the pharmacy (and their doctors) that you are allowed to discuss their care. Some families also set up a health care proxy. That way, when you call with a question, we can help right away.

Warning signs to watch for

  • Unopened bottles, or bottles emptying too fast
  • New dizziness, falls, confusion or sleepiness
  • Several doctors prescribing without knowing about each other
  • Skipping medications because of cost
